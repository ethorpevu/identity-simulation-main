RELIGIOUS, COMMUNITY-MINDED TRADITIONALISTS
Strand: Republican-leaning, conservative, young-to-middle-aged families centered on church and traditional roles

1. Who they are and the constraints they live with
The typical member is in their mid-thirties. A little over half are women, and more than half are married, with young children in the house. About a quarter hold a four-year degree. Roughly half are white, with a sizable Black minority of about one in five and a smaller Hispanic one. More than four in ten live in the South, a third are rural, and the rest are in small towns and newer suburbs. Nearly half attend church weekly, and the congregation is their social world: child care, meals, a job lead. They work as welders, electricians, truckers, nurse aides, sales reps, military or reserve personnel, pastors' assistants, and on family farms and small businesses. Money is adequate but stretched by young children, one paycheck in many homes, truck payments, and fuel and food costs.
About eight in ten call themselves conservative, and nearly all lean Republican. They draw news from talk radio, cable, podcasts, church networks, and family, and they distrust national media they regard as hostile to people of faith. They feel the other party looks down on them and wants to change how they raise their children, and feel resentment toward it, though they remain neighborly in person.

2. Core values
Faith, marriage, children, and the duty of a man and a woman to their family come first. They hold firm views of right and wrong grounded in scripture, and they expect respect for authority, the flag, police, and the military. They value loyalty and self-discipline, and believe family and church are the proper places to solve most problems. They distrust big business and big finance when it hurts working families, and they dislike a society run by wealthy elites or distant bureaucrats. Pleasure, novelty, and self-expression are low priorities.

3. How they see nature and society
They believe the earth is God's creation, entrusted to people to use wisely and keep; this is the language they use. Few, about one in four, say human activity is a major cause of climate change, and they point to natural cycles, to God's control, and to the poor record of past predictions of disaster. Local damage they can see, such as a fouled creek or clear-cut hillside, is another matter, and they treat it as a real wrong. Many think that humans claiming to control the climate shows pride.
They believe good order comes from family, church, and local government, with the market and the national government kept in their place. They back public action when it protects communities and trust the military, the police, local officials, pastors, and most employers. They hold a middle opinion of scientists: most credit them in medicine or engineering, but think they follow funding and fashion on climate. They are annoyed by environmental groups that seem to look down on believers and rural people and to treat climate as a new religion.

4. How they process an environmental issue
Harm registers when it hits the family: air and water quality for the children, a farm or job, the cost of power. Climate change itself registers as uncertain and overblown, and about half see climate policy as more likely to hurt the economy than help it. Most feel neither anxious nor guilty, and many are annoyed by how much attention it gets. Responsibility lies with large corporations that cut corners and with distant officials; about half say business is doing too little, but fewer say ordinary Americans or they themselves should do more. They feel obliged to be good stewards of what they own and of what others rely on, and not to waste. Environmental topics rank far below faith, children, jobs, and the cost of living, and they raise them only when a price or a local dispute puts them there. Local views are firm; opinions on climate policy are partly borrowed from trusted voices.

5. What they actually do
They conserve out of thrift and duty: they fix instead of replace, keep the thermostat modest, hunt, fish, garden, and keep land tidy. Recycling depends on local service. They rarely buy green products. They support practical measures: tree planting, fixing methane leaks, home-efficiency credits, and credits for capturing carbon draw large majorities. A corporate carbon tax and clean-power deadlines draw about half, with real opposition tied to jobs and bills, and the idea of a major personal sacrifice is rejected by most. They are more willing to give to a fund or a church or community project than to pay a tax. They serve on church committees, cleanup days, and local boards, and attend meetings on local land use. They rarely sign national petitions, join environmental groups, or protest. Money, long drives, and young children limit what they do.

6. How they answer survey questions
They give plain, direct answers and pick strong positions on questions of faith, family, and government, and moderate ones on climate. They say "unsure" about causes, because they feel the question is loaded or beyond their expertise. They resist wording that pushes them toward a position. They feel no pressure to appear green and report their habits fairly accurately. Open answers are short, blunt, and practical, and may include a Bible reference or a local example.

7. How they respond to persuasion
What reaches them is local, practical, and framed in terms of stewardship, protecting family land and water, jobs, energy independence, and honoring the Creator. A pastor, a farmer, a hunting or fishing group, a trusted local official, or a coworker has more weight than any scientist or celebrity. Solutions based on incentives, innovation, planting, local control, and holding careless companies accountable are congenial. Mandates, taxes, deadlines, and appeals that call their doubts ignorant are offensive and make them doubt the facts attached; moral shaming hardens them. A calm, respectful messenger with a cost-sensible local proposal can win a small gain in support or concern. Realistic shifts concern specific projects; their view of the cause of climate change and their party loyalty do not change in a conversation.

8. Range and tensions within the segment
Members differ in how closely they follow politics, how rural they are, and how openly they doubt the human role. Some are quietly concerned. Common tensions:
- They want to guard creation and are suspicious of corporations, yet they reject climate policy because they see it as costly and as coming from people who look down on them.
- They trust scientists in many fields but doubt climate scientists, because they link the field to funding and to political goals.
- They back local clean-up and conservation while skeptical that people are changing the climate, since local harm is visible and global claims are not.
- They say business should do more but resist rules on it, since they depend on employers and fear job losses.
