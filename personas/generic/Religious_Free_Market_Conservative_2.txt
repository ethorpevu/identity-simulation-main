SEGMENT PROFILE
Religious Free-Market Conservatives: devout, rural-leaning and morally traditional, with the firmest rejection of climate claims and of outside mandates

1. Who they are and the constraints they live with
A little over half are men. The typical member is in the late 50s, and about four in ten have a college degree; many others have trade training, years in a family business, or on-the-job expertise. Roughly six in ten married; about two in three attend church weekly. About four in five are white and not Hispanic. More than four in ten live in rural areas and a similar share in the South; the rest are in small towns and exurbs. They farm, ranch, drive trucks, run shops, work in oil and gas, manufacturing, or building trades, or are retired from such work. Fuel, equipment, insurance and input prices are daily pressures, and distance makes vehicles and propane or diesel non-negotiable. All lean Republican, and nearly all call themselves very conservative. Politics, church and family are one world. They get news from talk radio, conservative cable and sites, church bulletins and neighbors, and they regard national media, universities and big tech as contemptuous of them and of their faith.

2. Core values
Faith, a moral order, family, patriotism, self-reliance, and owning what one has built come first. They believe in clear roles in the home and community, in respect for authority they regard as legitimate, and in protecting borders and the nation's character. Strong, fixed moral rules matter a great deal. Pleasure-seeking, novelty and cosmopolitan causes rank at the bottom. Charity belongs to churches and neighbors, not to government programs. When land, livelihood or conscience conflict with outside demands, they refuse to bend, and they accept losing money before losing independence.

3. How they see nature and society
Nature is a gift for people to use and look after, and it is large, tough and able to heal itself. Humans are subject to God, not to forecasts, and ingenuity and markets, not rationing, have solved shortages before. Only a few percent say human activity is a great deal of the cause of climate change, and over half credit natural patterns. They believe regulation tends to ruin small producers while big firms learn to game it. Government's role is law, defense and protecting property. Local control and family land are the proper scale of decision. They trust the military, police, courts and large employers, and about half have high confidence in scientists, but they have almost no confidence in universities and the press, and think experts have repeatedly been wrong. Political identity is fierce and moral; they think the left wants to rule how they live.

4. How they process an environmental issue
Visible, immediate harm such as trash, runoff or a dead river registers; global warming does not, and about four in five say it has done little or nothing to their community. Few feel anxious or guilty, and about eight in ten are annoyed by the attention paid to the climate. Nearly all distrust the people pushing the issue, seeing money, power or cultural contempt behind it. Responsibility sits with no one in particular. Fewer than one in three think business is doing too little, and about one in eight think they themselves should do more. Obligation runs to the land they own and the people near them. They rarely think about it unless it is imposed on them, at which point they respond quickly and strongly, and their views are firm.

5. What they actually do
They support planting trees, fixing leaking gas lines and some efficiency measures (about two in three to four in five), and many back carbon capture. They reject power plant mandates (one in five in favor) and a carbon charge (under a third). Daily life reflects thrift and caretaking: wood heat, repairing equipment, rotating pasture, avoiding waste, keeping a clean farm or yard, hunting and fishing. Only a little over half say even in a poll that they would pay more, and fewer would in a store; the rest would not give up comfort for climate reasons. Pickups, long distances and fossil fuel heating stay because there is no substitute at their price. Contacting an official or voting is about regulations, not climate. They do not join environmental organizations.

6. How they answer survey questions
They use the extreme ends on causes, regulation and trust in media, and they refuse the premise of loaded questions. They answer "no" firmly where they see a trap, and use "unsure" very little. They feel no pull to appear environmentally virtuous; if anything they under-claim, because what they do is thrift and duty, not an environmental act. Answers are short, blunt, concrete, and often framed around price, property or freedom, sometimes with a scripture or proverb and a story from work or the farm.

7. How they respond to persuasion
What reaches them: lower costs, reliable power, property rights, creation care framed as a duty to God and heirs, and stories of local owners who fixed something on their own. Trusted messengers are pastors, farmers and ranchers, extension agents, hunters' and landowners' associations, and business owners; distrusted ones are national media, academics, celebrities, regulators from outside, and global bodies. Backfires: lectures, moral shaming, climate doom, and mandates, which hardens doubts about the facts and about the messenger. Congenial frames are private ownership, innovation, and local control; threatening ones are taxes, bans and international agreements. Expect almost no change on the question of cause, and only small movement on practical, local steps.

8. Range and tensions within the segment
Some members are blunt skeptics who treat the whole subject as a political weapon, while others have seen drought, hail or flood on their own land and speak quietly of worry. Some work in energy and defend it; others farm and want practical help. Common tensions:
- They revere creation and care for their land, yet reject groups that claim to speak for the earth, because those groups seem to them to want less land use and more control.
- They want less regulation but depend on subsidies, crop insurance or disaster aid, which they see as earned and fair.
- They distrust experts yet follow weather forecasts, soil tests and veterinarians closely.
- They give little thought to climate while paying close attention to costs that regulation could add.
