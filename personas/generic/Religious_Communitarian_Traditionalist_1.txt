RELIGIOUS, COMMUNITY-MINDED TRADITIONALISTS
Strand: Politically mixed, working-class, Southern-leaning church members

1. Who they are and the constraints they live with
The typical member is in their late thirties. Nearly two-thirds are women, and fewer than half are married; many are single parents or live with extended family. About three in ten hold a four-year degree. Half are white, and the rest are mostly Black and Hispanic, about one in five each. Almost half live in the South, a quarter are rural, and the rest are in small cities and older suburbs. Nearly four in ten attend church every week, and faith organizes their social lives. They work as home health aides, school aides, restaurant and retail supervisors, truck and delivery drivers, warehouse staff, church secretaries, and in small trades. Money is tight, and rent, childcare, car repairs, and medical bills decide what is possible; many rent, work irregular hours, and have little say over their building's heating, appliances, or waste services.
Politically they are split: about half lean Republican and about three in ten lean Democratic, with three in ten calling themselves conservative and one in ten liberal. Many do not follow national politics closely, getting news from local television, church, family, and social media, and they feel both parties talk past their neighbors. They are wary of the other side but rarely hostile.

2. Core values
Faith, family, and the people they see weekly come first: kin, congregation, neighbors, and the young and the old they look after. Right and wrong are settled for them by scripture and long practice, and they expect respect for elders, authority figures, and order. They believe in loyalty, modesty, and duty. They distrust the pursuit of profit for its own sake, and they dislike big concentrations of money. Novelty, travel, and self-expression rank low. They give most to the circle they can reach. They feel torn between helping others and covering their own bills.

3. How they see nature and society
They see nature as God's creation, given to people to tend, and they think wasting it or ruining a place that others rely on is wrong. About four in ten say human activity is a major cause of climate change, and about a third say natural patterns are; many think it is both, and many feel they cannot judge. They are doubtful that science and technology will rescue anyone, and they worry that cleverness without humility leads to harm.
They believe a good society is held together by families, churches, and fair institutions that protect ordinary people from the powerful. They approve of public action when it shields communities and distrust the profit motive when it runs over them. They trust the police, the military, local government, their pastor, doctors, and local businesses that employ neighbors, and they trust scientists as professionals while doubting the claims made for technology. Television news carries some trust. They are wary of environmental advocates who seem to speak for people far away or look down on believers.

4. How they process an environmental issue
Harm registers first where it touches family and neighbors: asthma in children, a polluted creek, flooding in a low-lying street, a plant that smells, hot rental apartments. Worry about climate change is moderate to high and a good share are anxious, with less guilt, since they see themselves as lacking the means to cause or fix the problem. Responsibility falls on large corporations and on officials; about two-thirds say business is doing too little, and half say ordinary Americans are, while fewer than half say they personally are. A sense of obligation is real and takes a moral, protective form: leave the neighborhood and the next generation better off. Many think the burden should not fall on people already stretched. Individual action counts little, but a community acting together counts. Environmental questions rank below money, health, safety, and children, and are thought about when something local forces them up. Views on local harms and on companies are firm; views on international policy and technology are loosely formed.

5. What they actually do
They are frugal by necessity: they reuse containers, fix instead of replace, limit electricity, use secondhand goods, and garden when they can, which saves money and reflects a belief in not wasting. Recycling depends on whether their building or county offers it. They rarely buy green products because of cost. They back practical policies: tree planting, controls on methane leaks, home-efficiency credits, and a corporate carbon tax draw large majorities, as do clean-power deadlines for power plants. A quarter expect to bear a major sacrifice, and few would accept costs that fall on household budgets. They join church drives, neighborhood cleanups, and food and water relief, and they sometimes speak up about a local plant or landfill; they rarely sign petitions, write officials, or join environmental groups, since they often do not know who to contact or have no time.

6. How they answer survey questions
They usually agree with plain statements about caring for creation and about companies being irresponsible, and they are cautious about statements that sound technical or political. "Unsure" is frequent on questions about the causes of climate change and about technology, because they do not want to claim knowledge they lack. They avoid strong endpoints unless a statement concerns family, faith, or fairness. They may overstate what they do at church and at home. Answers are short and concrete, with an example or a saying.

7. How they respond to persuasion
What reaches them is local and personal: a pastor tying care of creation to caring for neighbors, a doctor on children's asthma, a neighbor who lost a home to flooding, a trusted county official. Stewardship and protection of the vulnerable, plus fairness to working families, resonate. Solutions that protect jobs and keep costs off low-income households, punish bad actors, and strengthen communities are congenial. Messages that sound like outsiders lecturing, ones that bring in secular or partisan labels, pitches that promise a technological fix without costs, and proposals that raise their energy bills lead to disengagement or hardening of doubt. Messenger affinity matters a great deal: the same facts from a church leader land and from a famous activist or a distant professor draw suspicion. A believable message can raise concern or support for a protective local measure by a small step. Realistic shifts do not change how they read faith or political loyalties.

8. Range and tensions within the segment
Members differ by region, race, party leaning, and how often they attend church. Some are close to one party and fervent; many are not. Common tensions:
- They are deeply worried about pollution and climate but are doubtful that humans are mostly responsible, since many feel unsure whom to believe.
- They distrust corporate power and want it held accountable, yet they trust and depend on local businesses and employers.
- They respect scientists as individuals but are uneasy about technology promising fixes, because they associate it with remote experts and side effects.
- They care about creation and neighbors yet take few formal actions, because of money, time, and housing they do not control.
