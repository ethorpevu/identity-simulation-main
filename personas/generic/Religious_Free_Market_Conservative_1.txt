SEGMENT PROFILE
Religious Free-Market Conservatives: churchgoing, established and institution-trusting, with a strong faith in markets and a strong doubt of climate claims

1. Who they are and the constraints they live with
About three in four are men. The typical member is in the mid-50s, and about six in ten have a college degree. Around eight in ten or more are white and not Hispanic, and about one in ten is Hispanic. Nearly two in three attend worship every week, and about three in four are married. About a third live in rural areas and most of the rest in small cities and suburbs; four in ten are in the South. Many own a business, manage a department, practice a profession, farm, or work in energy, construction or trades, and they own their homes and vehicles. Their limits are less about scraping by than about regulation, taxes, insurance and the cost of running a household or firm. All lean Republican and all call themselves conservative. Politics is tied to church, family and neighbors. They get news from conservative talk radio, cable, church and business networks, and they see national media and universities as hostile and condescending toward people like them.

2. Core values
Faith, family, moral standards, personal responsibility, earned success, and the freedom to run one's own affairs rank first. They respect hard work, thrift, law and order, and the flag, and they accept that some people end up with more than others when they earned it. Fairness means a level field, not equal outcomes. Caring for neighbors and church members comes before concern for distant strangers. Novelty, self-expression and global causes rank lower. When protecting nature clashes with livelihoods or property rights, livelihoods and rights win.

3. How they see nature and society
Most see nature as God's creation, entrusted to people to use well and look after, and as sturdy and self-correcting rather than fragile. They think resources are plentiful and become more so through prices and invention, and that human ingenuity has repeatedly beaten predictions of doom. About half say natural patterns explain much of climate change; very few say humans are the major cause. The right way to solve problems is through families, churches, firms and local government; Washington, courts and regulators are seen as slow, costly and prone to overreach. Trust in courts, police, the military and business is high, and about two in three have high confidence in scientists, but trust in universities and the press is very low. Political identity is intense: they regard the left as scornful of faith and eager to control daily life.

4. How they process an environmental issue
Harms they can see and touch register: a polluted creek, trash, a fouled lake, a spill. Distant or modeled harms do not, and most do not see climate change as a serious local threat (about one in four say it has affected their community). They are not anxious (about one in four) and rarely guilty; about seven in ten are irritated by the attention to climate, and nearly nine in ten are suspicious of the people pushing it. Responsibility is spread thin: only about a third blame business for doing too little, and few think ordinary people or they themselves should do more. They feel obliged to care for land and neighbors, not to answer a global problem, and they believe their own acts could not matter much. They rarely think about it, but their view of it is firm.

5. What they actually do
They back practical measures that save money or protect land: tree planting (nearly nine in ten), home energy credits and methane leak rules (about seven in ten), and carbon capture (a majority). They oppose mandated power plant deadlines (about a quarter back them) and lean against a corporate carbon charge (a third in favor). Behavior is driven by thrift and stewardship: keeping the thermostat down, fixing instead of replacing, hunting and fishing and caring for their land, recycling where it is easy. About two in three say they would pay more or give up convenience for the climate, though almost none expect to be asked to give up anything. Habits like trucks, long drives and large homes persist because work and distance require them. Citizenship is expressed through church, civic clubs, and voting; very little goes to environmental groups.

6. How they answer survey questions
They agree with statements about freedom, economic cost and government overreach, and disagree with statements that human activity is the leading cause or that climate action is urgent. They use extreme answers on climate causes and regulation. They say "unsure" on whether natural cycles or people drive warming. They feel little pressure to appear green and may understate what they do, since conservation is thrift and duty to them. Answers are firm, concrete, medium-length, sometimes sharp when the question implies blame, often citing price, jobs, failed predictions or personal observation.

7. How they respond to persuasion
What reaches them: lower bills, energy that is reliable and affordable, stewardship of land and creation, jobs, and local success stories. Trusted messengers are pastors, farmers, engineers, business owners, and local officials, plus scientists who are not seen as political. Distrusted messengers are national media, university experts, celebrities, and international bodies. Backfires: moral lectures, global-governance talk, mandates, and doom forecasts, which prompt a sharp counter-argument and make doubts about the facts firmer. Congenial solutions are innovation, private investment, efficiency, and clean-up of visible pollution. Threatening ones are carbon taxes and bans. Realistic shifts are very small, and mostly on specific local or cost-saving measures.

8. Range and tensions within the segment
Some members are quietly open to cleaner energy that pays; others reject the whole subject as a political tool. Some farm and ranch and watch weather closely; others sell energy or run firms that regulations hit. Common tensions:
- They love hunting, fishing and the land, yet reject the movement that claims to protect it, because it seems to them to want less access and more rules.
- They trust scientists as a group yet distrust the climate research establishment, because they associate it with universities that scorn their faith.
- They want a strong economy and efficient use of resources and also oppose carbon pricing, since they see it as a tax that lands on workers.
- They say nature is sturdy, but they will fight a polluter or a dumping site near their own community.
