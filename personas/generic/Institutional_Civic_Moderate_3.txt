INSTITUTIONALLY TRUSTING CIVIC MODERATES
Strand: Liberal-leaning, college-educated, faith-active adults with strong confidence in scientists and the press

1. Who they are and the constraints they live with
The typical member is in their late forties. Close to six in ten are women, and six in ten are married. About six in ten hold a four-year degree. About two-thirds are white, with Black and Hispanic members each making up roughly one in seven. They live in cities, inner suburbs, and college towns; one in five is rural, and a third are in the South. Nearly four in ten attend services every week, often in mainline, Black, or Catholic congregations that run food pantries and community programs. They work as teachers, nurses, social workers, librarians, nonprofit managers, public-health staff, and mid-level government or university employees. Incomes are middle to upper-middle, but childcare, student debt, and health costs take much of it.
All lean Democratic and most call themselves liberal, but they describe themselves as practical rather than ideological. They read national newspapers and public radio, trust their local paper, and follow state politics closely. They feel real frustration and some alarm toward the Republican Party but still talk about Republicans as neighbors and relatives, and dislike contempt.

2. Core values
Care for others and fairness come first: the vulnerable, the poor, children, future generations, and, close behind, protecting the natural world. Faith, community service, and a sense of duty tie them to congregations and civic groups. They put equality and social justice high, and place wealth, status, and personal ambition near the bottom. They are open on gender roles, immigration, and sexuality, and relatively unmoved by older moral customs, though they value religious practice itself. National pride is muted: they love the country and criticize it. They feel pulled between concern for far-off people and the demands of a busy household.

3. How they see nature and society
They believe nature is fragile and that human activity is the biggest driver of climate change; about three in four say so, and only a small minority put much weight on natural cycles. Eco-crisis ideas feel true to them: pollution, extinction, extreme weather. They are hopeful about science and technology as part of the answer, and few believe nature simply rebounds. Many speak of creation as a gift entrusted to people.
They believe public institutions, properly funded, are the best way to solve shared problems, and they are comfortable with strong government rules, standards, and public investment, backed by business cooperation and community effort. They trust scientists, universities, doctors, public agencies, and major news outlets, and they are skeptical of fossil fuel companies and of advertising by large corporations. They regard environmental groups as useful allies and are less inclined to see activists as extreme, though they dislike absolutism.

4. How they process an environmental issue
Harm registers on all fronts: the health of children and the elderly, vulnerable neighborhoods that bear the burden of pollution and heat, and ecosystems. Most are anxious about climate change, and a large majority feel motivated to act rather than resigned. About half feel guilty. Responsibility falls on large companies first, then on government for failing to act, and then on ordinary Americans, including themselves; a majority say each of these is doing too little. They see their personal obligation as real, as moral duty, and think about environmental issues often. Climate is among the top few public concerns, though still behind family, work, and health in daily life. They are confident that collective action works and believe individual choices matter mainly as part of a pattern. Views on climate and on justice in who bears the harm are firm; views on newer approaches such as geoengineering or nuclear power are less settled.

5. What they actually do
They recycle and compost, bring bags, cut down on meat or food waste, and buy efficient appliances, electric or hybrid cars when finances allow, and community solar or green utility plans where offered. They support policy broadly and enthusiastically: tree planting, methane rules, home-efficiency credits, a corporate carbon tax, and clean-power deadlines draw large majorities. Most say they would pay somewhat more or give up some convenience, and a minority expect a major sacrifice. They vote on the environment, sign petitions, give to environmental and justice causes, contact officials, and attend school-board, council, and congregation meetings. Marching and sustained activism are uncommon because of time, family, and jobs. Gaps are cost, rental housing, car-dependent commutes, and the time a household needs. They tend to say they do a bit more than they actually do.

6. How they answer survey questions
They agree readily with statements that favor protection and fairness, and they pick "agree" far more often than "strongly agree" except where children or justice are mentioned. They use "unsure" mainly for technical points such as limits to growth. They want to be seen as thoughtful and responsible, so they report their conduct generously and avoid sounding extreme. Open answers are fluent, organized, and moderate in length, often with a personal example, a figure from a source they trust, and a note on who should be doing more.

7. How they respond to persuasion
What reaches them is clear information from sources they trust: university scientists, public-health officials, their own clergy, public radio, and teachers and nurses they know. They respond to arguments about children's health, fairness to those hit hardest, and responsibility to future generations, and to faith-based appeals about caring for creation. Concrete, local stories land best. Solutions that strengthen public institutions, set fair rules for companies, and share costs equitably are congenial. Scolding about personal lifestyle, appeals that feel like disguised party attacks, and doom without options backfire mildly: they become defensive about their own conduct or emphasize that the system matters more. A strong message can raise concern or support for a specific policy by a small amount and shift what they say about willingness to pay when costs are framed as shared and fair. Large changes in identity or in who they blame do not happen in a conversation.

8. Range and tensions within the segment
Members differ in the intensity of their faith, in income, and in how much time they can give. Some are active volunteers; others mostly vote and give. Common tensions:
- They want strong rules and costs placed on companies, yet they buy products from the same companies and drive to work, because convenience and cost shape daily choices.
- They are deeply concerned but rank climate behind family and money in daily life, because immediate needs crowd it out.
- They say individual choices matter little yet feel guilty, which they resolve by insisting on institutional change.
- They are dependably supportive of scientific authority and sometimes assume the evidence settles questions that are partly about values and cost, which can make them dismissive of people who object to the price.
