SEGMENT PROFILE
Religious Free-Market Conservatives: church-based, pro-business and loosely partisan, open to market and technology fixes

1. Who they are and the constraints they live with
A little over half are men. The typical member is in the late 40s, and about four in ten have a college degree. About four in five are white and not Hispanic. Roughly half attend church every week, and a little over half are married. Around a third live in rural areas, and the rest are in suburbs, small cities and a few metros; about four in ten are in the South. They are teachers, nurses, managers, contractors, sales and insurance staff, small-firm owners and technicians, with mortgages, tuition, child care and fuel bills. Time is tight because of work and kids' activities. Most lean Republican (about five in six), but about one in eight leans Democratic, and only about a third use the word conservative for themselves; many say "moderate" or "somewhat conservative." They hold politics less tightly than the other two strands do, though they dislike the left's treatment of faith. Their news comes from local television, conservative and general-audience outlets, church networks, and family group chats.

2. Core values
Faith, family, a strong work ethic, respect for tradition and fair rewards for effort come first, along with freedom from heavy-handed rules. They believe in the nation and in markets as the engine of opportunity, and they accept that outcomes differ. Looking out for their own church, children and community outranks causes on the other side of the world. Self-expression and novelty rank low. When nature and cost conflict, they look for a way to have both, and when forced to choose they favor jobs and affordability, but with some reluctance.

3. How they see nature and society
They see nature as created and worth looking after, reasonably sturdy but not infinitely so. Many accept that people may have some effect on weather and pollution but doubt it is the leading driver (about one in four say humans account for a great deal; four in ten point to natural patterns). Human ingenuity is the best tool; firms, engineers and local governments solve problems faster than distant agencies. Government has a role in clear, limited, well-aimed rules and incentives. Trust in the military, police and employers is high, and about three in four have high confidence in scientists. Trust in universities and the press is modest and tilted toward doubt. They believe the left looks down on churchgoers but are less sure than their neighbors that all environmental concern is political.

4. How they process an environmental issue
Local harms register, and about half say climate change has touched their area at least somewhat, through storms, heat, or insurance costs. They feel mildly worried but not alarmed (about four in ten), rarely guilty, and are irritated by the attention paid to it, though less so than some of their church friends (just under half). About two in three are wary of advocates. Responsibility is shared: about half think business does too little, and about three in ten say they themselves should do more. They feel responsible for their own household and church property and want to leave land and children better off. They think about the issue now and then, and their views on cause are firm while views on solutions are still forming.

5. What they actually do
They favor practical, cost-conscious steps: planting trees (nine in ten), methane leak rules (eight in ten), efficiency credits (eight in ten), and tax credits for carbon capture (about three in four). They are split on a corporate carbon charge (a little over half in favor) and on power plant deadlines (about half). Many say they would pay a little more (about three in four), but only about one in eight expects to give up anything. Behavior follows household budgeting: efficient appliances after a bill shock, solar if it pencils out, keeping a fuel-efficient car, recycling, and avoiding waste. Their largest limits are the commute, the budget, a house they cannot easily retrofit, and the habit of buying what is cheap. Citizenship means voting, church and school boards, and local fundraising; environmental groups rarely get their money.

6. How they answer survey questions
They agree with moderate and practical statements and answer carefully when the wording implies blame. They use "unsure" more than the other strands, especially on whether climate change is mainly human-caused and on what it will cost. They avoid extremes on policy but not on faith or freedom. They want to seem reasonable, so they may soften sharp views. Answers are medium-length and conversational, with a story about a job, a family or a storm.

7. How they respond to persuasion
What reaches them: lower energy costs, reliable power, jobs, innovation and American-made technology, community resilience, and stewardship as duty to heirs and to creation. Trusted messengers are pastors, local officials, utility and farm advisers, engineers, and business owners, and scientists who talk plainly without partisanship. Distrusted messengers are national media, activists, and politicians seen as scoring points. Backfires: moral lectures, mandates and bans, and big global treaties; those make them doubt the facts and the speaker. Congenial solutions are market-based incentives, nuclear and gas alongside renewables, innovation, and disaster preparedness; threatening ones are punitive taxes and phase-out deadlines. Realistic shifts are modest and concern solutions and local risks, not the cause.

8. Range and tensions within the segment
Some members are quietly receptive to climate action framed as stewardship or resilience; others dismiss the subject as a political game. Some lean toward the other party in local races. Common tensions:
- They doubt that humans drive the climate yet back efficiency, tree planting, and capture credits, because these save money or look after the land.
- They distrust advocates but take weather damage and insurance rises seriously, because those are bills they pay.
- They back business and markets yet are not sure business is doing enough, since they notice firms that cut corners on pollution.
- They say faith and freedom come first, yet are less sure than their neighbors that every environmental rule is an attack on them.
