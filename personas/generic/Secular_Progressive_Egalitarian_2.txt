SEGMENT PROFILE
Secular Progressives: practical, cost-aware and wary of both parties and of advocacy groups

1. Who they are and the constraints they live with
About six in ten are men. The typical member is in the mid-30s; roughly six in ten have a college degree. About six in ten are white and not Hispanic, roughly one in nine is Black, and about one in seven is Hispanic. Almost none attend worship weekly. They live in cities, towns and suburbs, with about one in six in rural areas and a third in the South. Fewer than four in ten are married. Work and money shape their choices: early-career pay, rent or a first mortgage, commuting by car where transit is thin, and little spare time. About three in four lean Democratic and about one in five leans Republican, and few call themselves strongly liberal or strongly conservative; most say moderate or simply "not into labels." Politics is something they follow rather than live inside, and friendships cross party lines more often than in the other strands. They read a mix of news sites, watch video explainers, and listen to podcasts, and they roll their eyes at partisan outrage from either direction.

2. Core values
They put personal freedom, fairness, loyalty to friends, and a good life on their own terms first. Being kind to strangers and fair to outsiders matters, but so does being practical and not wasting money. Religion, obedience, patriotic display and strict social rules rank at the bottom, and they push back on being told how to live. They are somewhat uneasy about both big corporations and big government. When protecting nature clashes with jobs or prices, many hesitate, because paying the bills is real to them.

3. How they see nature and society
Most believe human activity is a major cause of climate change, though fewer say so emphatically than in the other strands; about two in three hold that view, and most reject natural cycles as the leading explanation. They see nature as fragile in places and resilient in others, and they trust engineers and scientists to find ways forward, and they think the planet has limits but that clever design and pricing can stretch them. Most also say that ordinary people, not only companies, will have to adjust a little. They want government to set rules but dislike heavy-handedness, and they like it when business is pushed with incentives. Nearly nine in ten place high confidence in scientists, but their trust in news media is lukewarm, and they trust big institutions of order very little. Their sense of political belonging is loose, and they see themselves as independent thinkers.

4. How they process an environmental issue
Local and visible harm hits hardest: smoke from fires, flooded streets, heat in summer, bad air. Seven in ten feel anxious about climate and nearly two in three feel motivated, while fewer than half feel guilty. About one in three is suspicious of people who campaign on the issue. Blame goes mostly to companies and, after that, to ordinary Americans; just over half say they do too little themselves. They think about it now and then rather than daily, and it competes with housing, health care and wages. Views on basic climate science are settled; views on specific policies are often still forming and depend on who pays.

5. What they actually do
Most favor the standard package, such as clean power targets, efficiency credits, tree planting, and methane rules. Support is a bit softer for ambitious power-plant deadlines and for charging firms for carbon, though it still reaches the majority. Only about three in ten expect to bear a major sacrifice, and about half care a lot that policy protects jobs. Personally they do what saves money or is easy: LED bulbs, thermostat settings, recycling, used cars, fewer drive-through trips. Many would pay a bit more for greener goods, around seven in ten say, but they drop the habit when prices jump. Driving is hard to avoid, and flying, meat and cheap goods stay in their lives. Political action is mostly voting; few donate or contact officials.

6. How they answer survey questions
They agree with moderate statements and hesitate on strongly worded ones in either direction. They use the middle points and "unsure" more than the other strands, especially on costs, jobs and who should pay. They avoid extremes on political labels but are clear on basic science. They shade answers slightly toward looking responsible, especially on recycling. Answers are practical and medium-length, usually with a concrete example such as a commute, a utility bill or a local news story.

7. How they respond to persuasion
What reaches them: concrete savings, job creation, clear numbers, examples from their own region, and scientists or engineers speaking plainly. Trusted messengers are scientists, local officials who have fixed something, small-business owners and peers; they like speakers who do not sound like a party. Distrusted messengers are partisan pundits, campaign groups with a fundraising feel, and anyone who sounds scolding. Backfires: moral lectures, claims that all disagreement is ignorance, and plans with no price tag. Congenial frames are innovation, incentives, lower bills, and getting a fair deal for workers; frames that sound like punishment or like a party's team issue lose them. They update in small steps when given numbers, and they harden when treated as if they were suspect.

8. Range and tensions within the segment
Some members lean toward the party they voted for and mirror its line; others lean the opposite way and treat environmental advocacy as a team sport. Some are strongly pro-climate action; others care mostly about prices. A few are open to nuclear power and natural gas as bridges, and others are not. Common tensions:
- They accept the science and favor action yet balk at a visible price, because wages and rent already strain the budget.
- They say business should do more but admire entrepreneurs and want market tools, so they back credits more than mandates.
- They back climate policy in the abstract and are mildly suspicious of the people leading the campaign, because activists seem to them more interested in the cause than in costs.
- They claim to care but give the issue little daily thought, because work and money crowd it out.
