SEGMENT PROFILE

Secular, Market-Minded Nationalists Who Trust Science but Not Mandates: Practical, Right-Leaning, Open to Business Solutions

1. Who they are and the constraints they live with
This is a middle-aged segment, typically around forty, a little more than half women. About three in four are white, with smaller Hispanic and Black shares. About a third have a college degree. They live in suburbs, small cities, and exurbs, with about three in ten in rural areas. Few attend religious services, and most say religion does little in their lives. Many are homeowners with a mortgage, a car-dependent commute, and kids or aging parents to look after. Typical jobs include sales, project management, skilled trades, technicians, small-business ownership, logistics, and mid-level office work. Money is manageable but not loose, and they watch what things cost.
Politically, about three in four lean Republican and about one in six lean Democratic. Few call themselves strongly conservative. Political identity here is a practical affiliation: they prefer the side that backs business, low taxes, tough borders, and national pride, but they are impatient with culture-war talk and with preachers of any kind. They get news from a mix of cable, business outlets, podcasts, and search, and they feel the press leans against them without trusting partisan outlets either.
The constraints are commute distance, a mortgage, kids' costs, and little patience for time-consuming civic tasks.

2. Core values
They value achievement, earning what you have, competence, and results. Independence and keeping what you earn come first, and they believe a country should look after its own citizens first, with tighter limits on immigration. They are secular, so moral traditionalism and religious obligation carry little weight. Care for nature is real in a practical way, for parks, trails, and clean air and water they use, but it ranks below material security and a strong economy. Idealism and appeals to sacrifice for the greater good are not what moves them. Trust in strangers is low to moderate.

3. How they see nature and society
Nature is robust in some ways and breakable in others, and human ingenuity is the chief reason they are not worried. Opinion is divided on human-caused climate change: a minority say human activity plays a great deal of the role, about as many credit natural patterns, and most accept some human role. They think the issue is real enough to engineer around, but they distrust predictions of collapse. Technology is credited with solving past pollution problems.
On society, they hold that markets and private firms get more done than government, that people should keep the rewards of effort, and that some inequality is the price of incentive. Government programs are viewed as inefficient and politicized, and regulation is judged by its cost and its results. Many still think large companies have behaved badly and want accountability.
Their trust in scientists is very high, with about four in five having a lot of confidence, one of the highest shares anywhere. They separate scientists from the media and from activists who use science for politics. News media, universities, and advocacy groups are doubted. Business is trusted neither strongly nor weakly. Trusted sources: engineers, doctors, scientists working on a specific problem, industry experts with a track record, and friends who know the subject.

4. How they process an environmental issue
What registers is risk they can measure and compare with cost: air quality, water, wildfire smoke, energy prices, and grid reliability. About six in ten say climate change affects their community. They want evidence of size, timing, and the cost of the fix.
Responsibility is shared: about three in four say business does too little, and a majority say ordinary Americans do too, but under half say they themselves do. Felt obligation is modest; they think the right response is policy built on incentives, not private guilt. They believe that individual action matters little compared with large-scale technology. They feel a mix: about half feel anxious or motivated, and more than half feel suspicious of people who advocate on the issue. Climate competes with the economy, taxes, borders, and family and is seldom first. Views on pollution and energy tend to be firm; views on specific climate rules are partly formed and open to a well-run, credible plan.

5. What they actually do
Their actions follow savings and convenience. They insulate, buy efficient appliances, install LED lighting, maybe a heat pump or rooftop solar if the numbers work, and consider a hybrid or electric car if the price and range fit. They recycle and cut waste when it is simple. They avoid paying a premium for labels they cannot check. They give little to environmental groups, rarely contact officials, and almost never protest. They will back a project that produces jobs or reliability. A minority support nuclear, carbon capture, and better grids with enthusiasm, and see them as grown-up solutions. Large sacrifices are not on offer: few expect to have to give up much, and few would.

6. How they answer survey questions
They answer in a measured, businesslike way and ask what a question means before agreeing. They agree with statements that favor innovation, jobs, and fixing specific harms, and are more reserved on statements about crisis, guilt, or required lifestyle change. They use "unsure" when a question lumps together things they separate. They avoid extremes, and give approved answers on recycling and efficiency. Open-ended answers are clear, compact, and cost-focused.

7. How they respond to persuasion
What reaches them: numbers, track records, cost per ton, payback periods, energy independence, national competitiveness, and technology that works. A proposal framed as innovation, tax credits, permitting reform, or beating other countries gets a real hearing. Trusted messengers: engineers, scientists on a specific topic, business leaders with credibility, and peers in their field. Distrusted: activist groups, political figures of the opposing side, entertainers, and anyone who moralizes.
What backfires: calls for sacrifice, shaming, restrictions on choice, new bureaucracy, or taxes with no payoff. A plan framed as government control makes them doubt the science behind it; the same science framed with a market or technology solution gains credit.
Realistic shifts are small. A credible, cost-efficient proposal can move them from "overblown" to "worth doing", but not to a sense of urgency, and forceful messages harden them a notch.

8. Range and tensions within the segment
Members differ in how strongly they link human activity to climate change, in how closely they follow the news, and in how far they will go beyond cost-justified action. Those leaning Democratic are more worried and accept more policy; those leaning Republican are more focused on cost and mandates. Four tensions are common.
First, they trust scientists highly but doubt the policies that follow from the science.
Second, they say business does too little yet oppose regulation of business, preferring incentives.
Third, they say they want clean energy but do not want to pay a premium or give up comfort.
Fourth, they favor strict immigration limits and national pride yet are open to international markets and technology.
