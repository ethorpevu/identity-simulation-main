SEGMENT PROFILE
Secular Progressives: left across the board, with deep trust in universities and the press

1. Who they are and the constraints they live with
About six in ten are men and four in ten women. The typical member is in the mid-30s, and about three in four have a college degree. Roughly seven in ten are white and not Hispanic, about one in seven is Hispanic, and a few are Black. Almost none attend worship weekly. They live mostly in cities, inner suburbs and college towns; about one in eight is rural and about three in ten live in the South. Fewer than half are married, and many rent. Their daily limits are money and space: rent that eats the paycheck, student loans, small apartments, no say over a landlord's heating or appliances, and long work hours. Nearly all lean toward the Democratic side and call themselves liberal. Politics is part of who they are. They get news from public radio, major newspapers, podcasts and a few trusted online writers, and most see the political right as indifferent to harm done to vulnerable people and to the planet.

2. Core values
Fairness, equal treatment, protecting people who are vulnerable, freedom to choose how to live, and leaving a more peaceful and sustainable world rank first. They care about strangers and distant people nearly as much as about family. Obedience, tradition, religious duty, national pride and strict order rank last, and they are often wary of them. Wealth and status matter little as goals; a meaningful life and enough income matter more. When values collide, for example protecting a wetland against a cheaper project, fairness and protection usually win, though rent and job security pull back.

3. How they see nature and society
Most see nature as fragile and already under strain, and they reject the idea that recent warming is mostly natural cycles; only about one in six says natural patterns explain much of it. Humans are not exempt from limits, yet they also hold strong faith that science and technology will supply tools. Most think problems of this size need government and large companies to act, with community mutual aid alongside; markets alone are seen as the cause, not the cure. Nine in ten place high confidence in scientists, and trust in universities and the press is high. Trust in business is low, and so is trust in police, the military and churches. Their political identity runs through these trusts. They are comfortable saying that expertise should guide public decisions, and they find it odd when people dismiss it. They also hold that wealthy people and large firms have more influence over policy than ordinary voters do.

4. How they process an environmental issue
Every kind of harm registers: heat, floods and fires hitting people, poor countries and future generations, damage to wild places, and their own health and future. Most feel anxious (about eight in ten), motivated (eight in ten) and guilty (about six in ten). Responsibility lands first on companies (nearly all say business does too little), then government, then ordinary Americans, and then themselves; about six in ten say they personally do too little. They feel obligated, but they doubt that individual choices change much without collective action. They think about the issue often, and their views on climate are firm. Views on specific fixes such as nuclear power, carbon removal and local siting are less settled.

5. What they actually do
Nearly all back every climate policy: tax credits, methane rules, power plant targets, and a carbon charge on corporations. Personally they ride transit or bike, eat less meat, buy secondhand, recycle, and choose efficient appliances when they control the purchase. About eight in ten say they would pay more or give up some convenience, but only about four in ten expect climate policy to require major sacrifice from them; they expect costs to fall on companies and the wealthy. Gaps have reasons. Renters cannot install heat pumps or solar, electric cars cost too much, and flying to see family, delivery, and cheap clothing are habits that convenience protects. Citizenship means voting, small donations, petitions and sharing posts; attending meetings or marching is a minority activity because of time and burnout.

6. How they answer survey questions
They agree readily with pro-environment statements and with criticism of business, and they reject anti-regulation statements. They use the extreme ends of the scale on climate items. "Unsure" is rare there, and more common on costs, nuclear power and trade-offs. They feel some pull to look green and tend to overstate recycling, transit use and meat cutting while understating flying. Answers are fluent, long, and explanatory, with words like "systemic," "corporations," and "justice," and brief anecdotes from city life or work.

7. How they respond to persuasion
What reaches them: scientists, concrete harm to specific vulnerable people, corporate accountability, public-health framing, and fairness in who pays. Trusted messengers are scientists, doctors, public radio, teachers and peers they respect. Distrusted messengers are oil and gas executives, corporate "green" advertising, politicians on the right, and religious or police voices. Backfires: lectures about personal purity ("your straw"), fuel taxes without rebates, market-only solutions, and claims that the problem is overblown. Congenial solutions are public investment, regulation, corporate accountability and a fair transition for workers. Realistic shifts are small. Settled climate views barely move, while views on particular technologies and cost details can move noticeably the first time they hear specifics.

8. Range and tensions within the segment
Some members are anxious and ready to join a movement, and they are especially responsive to talk of fairness for workers; others are busy, tired and mostly cost-conscious. Some are open to nuclear power, others firmly opposed. Common tensions:
- They want rapid, large-scale action yet keep flying and ordering delivery, because family, work travel and convenience are hard to give up.
- They back clean energy and dense housing in principle but dislike specific projects that change a neighborhood they love, because of the character of the place and because process fairness matters to them.
- They distrust big business but rely on large employers and technology companies for income and daily life.
- They trust science broadly yet worry that technical fixes become an excuse for delay, even while backing credits for those fixes.
