SEGMENT PROFILE

Politically Mixed Adults Who Trust Almost No One: Young, Working-Class, Suspicious of Companies and Officials Alike

1. Who they are and the constraints they live with
This is a younger, working-age segment, typically in the mid-thirties. About two in three are women. It is ethnically mixed: roughly a third Hispanic, a quarter Black, a third white. Only about one in five has a college degree. Most live in cities and suburbs, about three in ten in rural areas, four in ten in the South. Many rent. Jobs tend to be retail, food service, home health, warehousing, delivery, child care, and gig work, with irregular hours and little cushion. About a quarter attend religious services weekly.
Politically they are the least settled of any strand: about half lean Republican, about a fifth lean Democratic, and the rest lean toward neither. Few call themselves strongly liberal or conservative. Political identity here means feeling unrepresented: politicians of both parties talk well and deliver nothing. News comes from social media, short video, local TV, and group chats; no outlet is theirs.
The constraints are money, time, and control: they rarely choose their housing, appliances, or commute, and one repair bill can erase a month's plans.

2. Core values
What matters most is getting by and keeping their own people safe and secure. Family, loyalty to close friends, and plain honesty rank high. Fairness is practical: people who work hard should not be squeezed by people who rig the game. Traditional views on family, behavior, and borders are noticeable, and many want firmer rules. Concern for strangers, abstract causes, and the planet ranks lower. Distrust of other people is the strongest feature: you can't be too careful, and anyone offering help probably wants something.

3. How they see nature and society
Nature is big and old, and not obviously fragile. Most accept that humans pollute and that local air, water, and land can be wrecked, especially near plants, highways, and landfills. Fewer accept that human activity is the chief driver of climate change: a minority say it plays a great deal of the role, and about a third credit natural patterns. Claims that technology will fix things get little credit; promised fixes have arrived late, cost more, and helped someone else.
They are not market enthusiasts. Most distrust big companies and want them held to account, and few think competition automatically helps ordinary people. But they have little faith in government; officials are seen as captured, slow, or incompetent. So the ones who should fix problems are the ones who caused them, and they won't.
Trust in sources is low across the board. About half have a lot of confidence in scientists, far less than in most segments, often meaning "some scientists, if no one is paying them." National news, universities, large nonprofits, and corporate announcements are doubted. Trusted sources are family, coworkers, neighbors, a pastor or local figure known personally, and their own eyes.

4. How they process an environmental issue
Harms that register are those they can see, smell, or pay for: contaminated tap water, heat in an apartment with no air conditioning, flooding on the block, asthma, gas prices. About six in ten say climate change affects their community. Distant ecological harm registers weakly.
Responsibility goes to big business first (about three in four say companies do too little) and to officials who let them off. A majority say ordinary Americans do too little, but they rarely include themselves; fewer than half say they personally do. Felt obligation is weak: "I'm not the one making the mess." They believe their own actions won't matter, and that belief is firm. Feelings are mixed: about half anxious, about half motivated, a majority suspicious of the people pushing the issue. Climate is rarely on their mind; money, work, safety, and family come first. Views on local pollution are firm. Views on climate policy are mostly unformed and get built on the spot from who is asking and what they stand to pay.

5. What they actually do
They save money, so many do low-cost things: reuse containers, buy secondhand, fix rather than replace, switch off lights, limit driving when gas is high. This is thrift and habit, not environmental motive. They recycle where the building provides bins. Greener products are occasional; many say they would pay a little more, but purchases follow the budget and what the nearest store carries. Joining groups, signing petitions, contacting officials, and attending meetings are rare: they doubt it works and don't know the organizers. Voting is irregular. Protest is almost nonexistent. Attitudes and actions are loosely connected; cost, context, and habit explain most of what they do.

6. How they answer survey questions
Most pick the cautious middle or say they are unsure when they haven't thought about something, and they wonder if the question is a trick or who will use the answer. Many agree with statements that blame companies or promise relief without cost, and agree less with statements asking them to give something up. Answers are short, informal, and concrete. They seldom use scale extremes except on blaming business. In open-ended answers they tell a short story about a bill, a landlord, or a company.

7. How they respond to persuasion
What reaches them: concrete local stories, named causes, costs placed on polluters instead of customers, jobs, lower bills, cleaner water or air in their own neighborhood. "The companies pay for it" and "this gets rid of what's making your kids sick" earn a hearing. Trusted messengers are neighbors, relatives, a nurse or tradesperson speaking from experience, and local figures with no stake. Distrusted: politicians in either party, large corporations, national media, celebrities, nonprofits asking for money, and experts who talk down.
What backfires: guilt, lectures, urgency, calls to sacrifice. Fees at the pump or on the bill read as a trap, and a far-off plan suggests the costs will land on them while sponsors profit. Congenial frames: holding companies accountable, cutting bills, fixing local infrastructure. Threatening frames: higher prices, restricted choices, trusting a new program.
Realistic shifts are small. A credible local example might move someone from indifference to mild interest. One message does not convince them of climate claims, and a forceful one hardens them.

8. Range and tensions within the segment
Members differ in politics, in how far they believe human activity changes the climate, and in what they'll accept. Those leaning Republican are more doubtful of climate claims and policy costs; those leaning Democratic are more open to the climate case but doubt it will be carried out fairly; unattached members are the most undecided. Four tensions are common.
First, they want companies to pay but expect costs to be passed on, so they back a policy in principle and doubt it in practice.
Second, they favor many specific measures in the abstract yet believe government will not deliver them.
Third, many say they care and would pay more, yet behavior follows price and convenience because the budget leaves no room.
Fourth, they hold traditional views on family and borders yet distrust business and the party establishments that claim those views, so no party feels like theirs.
