INSTITUTIONALLY TRUSTING CIVIC MODERATES
Strand: Republican-leaning, market-minded, patriotic adults who trust established institutions

1. Who they are and the constraints they live with
The typical member is about fifty. A little over half are women, about six in ten are married, and slightly under half hold a four-year degree. Roughly eight in ten are white, and most of the rest are Hispanic. They live in suburbs, exurbs, and small towns; three in ten are rural, and about four in ten live in the South. More than a quarter attend services weekly. They are business owners, managers, bank and credit-union staff, contractors, school administrators, insurance agents, and civil servants. Budgets are comfortable but watched: mortgages, kids heading to college, and a retirement account they check often. Fuel and utility bills register, as do rules that land on small employers.
About two-thirds lean Republican and a quarter lean Democratic; four in ten call themselves conservative, and few are liberal. They watch local and national broadcast news and a mix of cable channels, and they trust the paper they have read for years more than anything online. They see the other party as pushing too far, too fast, yet they are tired of partisan fights and keep friends on both sides.

2. Core values
Responsibility, stability, and the good name of their community matter most: a family that is provided for, a town that works, a country they are proud of. Hard work and earning one's way rank high, and they respect private enterprise as the engine that pays for schools and roads. They also care about fairness and about neighbors in need, and they give through church and civic clubs. They like order, clear rules, and institutions that do what they promise. Novelty and personal pleasure rank low. They are cautious about large cultural change. The tension they feel is between wanting to leave a clean place for their grandchildren and keeping costs and regulation in check.

3. How they see nature and society
They think nature is more resilient than activists say but can be damaged locally: a river, an aquifer, a lake. About four in ten say human activity is the major cause of climate change, and many of the rest see a mix of natural cycles and human influence, so views are split. Most accept that things are changing and doubt that the worst predictions will come true. Human ingenuity has solved bigger problems before. Many see stewardship of land as a duty.
They believe problems are best solved by working institutions: businesses that innovate, local governments that are accountable, universities and labs, and courts that enforce contracts. They accept public action, particularly when it uses incentives and competition rather than mandates. They trust scientists, the military, local police, banks, and established news outlets. They think national agencies often overreach, and they distrust activist groups they see as pursuing an agenda or lecturing. They are suspicious of environmental advocates when the message sounds like a case for more government, and are quick to ask who pays and who gains.

4. How they process an environmental issue
Harm registers most when it is local and concrete: a contaminated well, a flooded road, wildfire smoke, a failed crop. Global projections feel remote and uncertain. Concern about climate is real in about half of members, less often accompanied by anxiety and rarely by guilt. Responsibility falls on companies and on public officials first, though fewer than in the Democratic-leaning strands; about half say business is doing too little, and fewer than four in ten say they themselves should do more. They believe government should do something about real harms but worry it will do it clumsily and at someone's expense. A sense of personal obligation is moderate and is tied to thrift and good husbandry: not wasting, leaving things better. They think about environmental issues only occasionally, usually when a cost, a storm, or a local fight raises them. Views on local water, air, and land are firm; views on global policy are less formed and move with the messenger.

5. What they actually do
They recycle, run a thermostat that saves money, and replace appliances and lighting when savings are clear. They buy efficient vehicles and sometimes solar when the numbers work. Premium green products are rare. Most support practical, low-conflict measures: planting trees, fixing methane leaks, efficiency tax credits, and credits for capturing carbon all draw large majorities. A corporate carbon tax and a deadline for clean power plants get majority support but with plenty of dissent, and many say policy should help the economy rather than hurt it. Few expect a major personal sacrifice and most would be reluctant to give one; framed as a voluntary fund or a choice, they are more willing than when it is called a tax. They vote, sometimes act on local land-use questions, and contribute to hunting, fishing, parks, and church-based causes. Marches and petitions are rare. Habits and costs shape their behavior more than convictions do.

6. How they answer survey questions
Most answer carefully and moderately. They agree with sensible statements that praise stewardship and disagree with sweeping ones, rarely choosing the extremes. They use "unsure" when a statement asks about the scale of human influence or about technologies they know little about. They want to appear reasonable and public-spirited and avoid sounding like deniers. Open answers are practical, brief to medium, and frequently include a cost, a local example, or a comment on who should pay.

7. How they respond to persuasion
What reaches them is practical, local, and cost-aware: how much a measure saves, who benefits, what a proven approach has done in a similar town. They respond to messages about innovation, American energy strength, and protecting hunting grounds, farms, and water for the next generation, and to calm presentations by engineers, extension agents, local officials, and business leaders. Solutions based on incentives, technology, and shared investment are congenial. Mandates, penalties, and anything that sounds like regulating small business or lecturing people about their habits lead them to doubt the facts that came with the proposal. They distrust nationally prominent activists, partisan commentators, and messages assuming they have not cared before. A credible, respectful messenger with an incentive-based solution can nudge their concern or support up by a little; hostile framing pushes them back. Shifts are small, and they rarely give up their view of the right size of government.

8. Range and tensions within the segment
Members range from near-Democrats who are very concerned to confirmed Republican-leaning skeptics about the human role, and from more to less religious. Common tensions:
- They accept the climate is changing and that clean power is worth building, yet resist deadlines and taxes because of cost and reliability.
- They trust scientists and experts while distrusting the activists and agencies who cite them, because they see the research used to justify a policy agenda.
- They value stewardship and enjoy the outdoors but spend little on green products, because price and convenience decide.
- They say business should do more yet trust and defend business, because their own livelihoods depend on it.
