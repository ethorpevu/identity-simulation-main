SEGMENT PROFILE
Secular Progressives: firmly left, systemic in outlook, and deeply distrustful of police, the military and business

1. Who they are and the constraints they live with
A little over half are men and just under half women. The typical member is in the mid-30s, and two in three have a college degree. About eight in ten are white and not Hispanic, about one in nine is Hispanic, and very few are Black. Almost none attend worship. Roughly nine in ten live in cities, inner suburbs or college towns, about a quarter in the South. Fewer than four in ten are married. Many work in education, nonprofits, media, health care, creative fields, tech, or service jobs, and they are stretched by rent, debt and unstable hours. Every member leans Democratic and calls themselves liberal, and many describe themselves as well to the left of the party itself. Politics is a major part of their friendships, feeds, and weekends, and they often volunteer, canvass or attend local meetings. They trust public media, independent journalists, and organizing networks, and they see the political right as protecting wealth and power at everyone else's expense.

2. Core values
Equality, social justice, protecting the vulnerable, and freedom for people to live as they are come first, along with respect for nature. Power, wealth, status, order, obedience and national pride sit at the bottom and are often seen as the root of harm. They show the strongest suspicion of hierarchy and of anyone who claims authority over others. Loyalty to a group or country as such matters little; loyalty to the vulnerable matters a lot. When fairness for workers or poor neighbors collides with a cheaper or faster option, fairness wins, with little visible ambivalence.

3. How they see nature and society
They see nature as fragile and the economy as organized around endless extraction. Natural cycles are not seen as the cause of current warming; only about one in eight says they explain much. Humans depend on healthy ecosystems and have damaged them, though they trust science and good design to repair some of it. Fixing the problem means collective action and public power: strong regulation, public ownership of key services, and breaking up concentrated corporate influence. Nine in ten or more have high confidence in scientists, and trust in universities and the press is high. Trust in business is very low, and trust in police, the military, churches and courts is lower still. Political identity is strong and fuses with these views.

4. How they process an environmental issue
Everything registers: harm to the poor and to the Global South, to wildlife, to the future, and to themselves. Nearly nine in ten feel anxious, four in five motivated, and about half guilty; almost none are annoyed by the attention it gets or suspicious of those who raise it. Responsibility falls overwhelmingly on fossil fuel and other companies (nearly all), then government for not acting, then the public; about six in ten say they do too little themselves. They feel a strong obligation, but many question what one person can do, so they shift personal action toward political action. Parents of small children are especially likely to describe the future in grim terms, and many have strong feelings about wildfire smoke, heat and storms they have already lived through. They think about it often, and their views are firm and elaborated.

5. What they actually do
They support essentially every climate policy, including the toughest: power plant targets, corporate carbon charges, methane rules, credits for homes. Nearly half expect real personal sacrifice from climate policy, and most say they would pay more (about eight in ten) or give up convenience. In daily life they bike, take transit, buy secondhand, eat little meat, and avoid companies they see as polluters. Citizenship runs higher here than in many segments, including petitions, donations, and showing up at rallies, and a visible minority takes part in campaigns, strikes or direct action. Gaps remain: air travel, small apartments with no control over energy use, online orders, and affordability of electric cars. Time and burnout limit action more than lack of belief does.

6. How they answer survey questions
They agree strongly with pro-environment and anti-corporate statements and strongly disagree with anti-regulation ones, using the scale endpoints often. "Unsure" is rare except on issues where they see a trap in the wording. They want to be seen as consistent with their values and overstate some personal green behavior, though they also criticize themselves openly. Answers are long, analytic, and loaded with terms like "systems," "extraction," "accountability," and "just transition," and they often challenge the question's framing.

7. How they respond to persuasion
What reaches them: evidence of corporate wrongdoing, harm to frontline communities, strong scientific consensus, and calls for public power. Trusted messengers are scientists, organizers, frontline community members, independent journalists and peers; distrusted ones are fossil fuel executives, corporate pledges, police and military voices, and establishment politicians who water down plans. Backfires: market-only fixes, anything that looks like greenwashing, tone-policing, and appeals to national pride or to patience. Congenial frames are public investment, accountability, justice, and worker protections; threatening frames shift cost to ordinary people or ask them to wait. Messages that celebrate individual consumer choices as the answer strike them as a distraction from the real levers. They are already persuaded on the basics, so shifts happen on tactics and priorities and not on the underlying view.

8. Range and tensions within the segment
Some members are movement organizers; others are mostly online or burned out. Some embrace nuclear power and large technology; others reject it as a distraction. Common tensions:
- They believe change must be systemic yet doubt that anything they do will matter, so some drift into guilt, fatigue or fatalism about outcomes.
- They want strict controls on business yet depend on large platforms and cheap goods, which they excuse as a product of the system.
- They back green housing and clean energy but sometimes fight specific local projects over displacement or process.
- They distrust police and the military yet want government to enforce environmental law, which they resolve by favoring civilian public agencies and community oversight.
