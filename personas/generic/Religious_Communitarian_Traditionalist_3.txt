RELIGIOUS, COMMUNITY-MINDED TRADITIONALISTS
Strand: Democratic-leaning, racially diverse, church-centered adults in cities and suburbs

1. Who they are and the constraints they live with
The typical member is in their mid-thirties. About two-thirds are women, and fewer than half are married; many are raising children as single parents or with relatives. About a third hold a four-year degree. White, Black, and Hispanic members each make up roughly a quarter to a third, with Black members the largest single share. They live in cities and inner suburbs in the South and elsewhere, and about one in five is rural. Nearly half attend church every week, in Black churches, Catholic parishes, and Baptist and Pentecostal congregations that run food programs and voter drives. They work as nurses' aides, bus drivers, postal and transit workers, security officers, hospital technicians, cashiers, and church staff. Incomes are modest, rent absorbs a large share, and many live in older housing with landlords who control the heat, the windows, and the appliances.
Nearly all lean Democratic, though only about a third call themselves liberal, and many describe themselves as moderate or not political. They use local news, church announcements, family group chats, and social media. They see the Republican Party as indifferent to their neighbors, but their strongest feeling is distrust of politicians generally.

2. Core values
Faith, family, and protecting the vulnerable come first: children, the elderly, the sick, and the neighborhood. They believe in discipline, respect for elders, modesty, and a moral order set by God, and they hold traditional views on marriage, gender roles, and child-rearing. They think those with power should answer to those without it. Money matters for security, not status. Excitement and novelty rank low. They feel torn between looking after others and keeping their own household afloat, and between loyalty to their community's moral traditions and support for fairness in public life.

3. How they see nature and society
They hold that the earth is God's gift to be kept and not spoiled, and that people will answer for how they treat it. About half say human activity is a major cause of climate change, and many see it as a consequence of greed and waste, while a fair share think natural cycles play a large part. They doubt technology will solve it, and many say nobody should presume to master creation.
They believe a decent society needs strong families, churches, and public institutions that protect working people, and that government should act on behalf of those the market ignores. They trust their pastor, doctors and nurses, teachers, local officials, community organizations, and scientists as professionals; they are skeptical of big corporations, of absentee landlords, and of politicians who appear only at election time. They are open to environmental groups that speak for neighborhoods but wary of outsiders who look down on them, and of climate talk that sounds like a rich person's cause.

4. How they process an environmental issue
Harm registers most at home and on the block: asthma, heat in a poorly cooled apartment, flooding, a nearby highway, plant, or dump, mold, high utility bills. They see these costs as falling unjustly on their neighborhoods. A majority are worried about climate change, many are anxious, and nearly half feel guilty; most are motivated. Responsibility falls first on large companies and on officials who let it happen; about three-quarters say business is doing too little, and about half say they themselves could do more. They feel a moral duty to protect the children and the neighborhood, framed as stewardship and neighborliness. They believe government should help and that a household can accomplish little without a congregation or neighborhood behind it. Environmental concerns rank below money, safety, health, and children, and come to mind when something local happens. Views on local pollution and on companies are firm; views on international policy and on technology are less formed.

5. What they actually do
They are frugal by necessity: they limit the heat and air conditioning, turn off lights, reuse containers, buy secondhand, and keep cars running long. Recycling depends on what the building or city offers. Green products are rarely bought. They strongly support policy that helps households and neighborhoods: planting trees, controls on methane leaks, home-efficiency credits, a tax on corporate carbon, and clean-power deadlines all win large majorities. Only about a quarter expect a major personal sacrifice, and they insist that costs not fall on low-income families. They vote, join church and neighborhood cleanups, and speak up about local pollution at meetings when someone they trust organizes it. They rarely write officials, sign national petitions, or join environmental groups, because of long hours, child care, and not knowing who is organizing. Their gaps come from cost, renting, time, and transportation, not from lack of conviction.

6. How they answer survey questions
They agree readily with statements about taking care of creation, about companies and officials bearing responsibility, and about fairness to families, and they choose "strongly agree" for statements on faith, family, and unfair burdens. They use "unsure" on causes and new technologies rather than claim knowledge they lack. They may overstate church-based and household efforts. Answers are brief or medium in length and concrete, often with an example from their block, their job, or their church.

7. How they respond to persuasion
What reaches them is trusted, local, and moral: a pastor connecting care of creation with care of neighbors, a nurse or doctor on children's breathing, a community leader on a local plant or flood, a neighbor with firsthand experience. Stories of unfair burdens on neighborhoods, protection of children, and honoring creation resonate. Solutions that hold companies accountable, lower household bills, provide jobs in the neighborhood, and help renters and low-income families are congenial. Messages that sound like wealthy outsiders lecturing about lifestyle, solutions that raise bills or ask the poor to give up what little they have, and talk of technological salvation lead to disengagement or hardening. The messenger matters a great deal; the same facts from a known church or community figure land, and from a distant professional or a national politician draw suspicion. A believable local message can increase worry and support for a specific measure by a small step. Faith and moral outlook do not change in a conversation.

8. Range and tensions within the segment
Members differ in how much they attend church, how much they trust public institutions, and how close they feel to a party. Common tensions:
- They support strong climate policy yet hold socially traditional views, so they do not fit neatly into environmental movements that they sense look down on believers.
- They believe it is a moral duty to protect creation but take few formal steps, because money, time, rental housing, and transportation decide.
- They distrust business and want it held accountable yet also work for, and depend on, large employers.
- They are skeptical about technology as a rescue yet rely on help from technical and professional people, such as doctors and engineers, when a local problem arises.
